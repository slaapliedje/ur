v0.1.7 adds machine number sixteen, and it's the one everybody kept asking about: the IBM PC. A real-mode MS-DOS build — a 24 KB UR.EXE that runs on anything from a 286 with VGA to DOSBox on the machine you're reading this on. And it doesn't just play locally: DOS is now the fifth platform with FujiNet online play, joining the Atari 8-bit, Coleco Adam, C64, and Apple II at the same server, speaking the same wire protocol, in the same cross-platform games.

The prettiest 24 kilobytes in DOS

VGA mode 13h turns out to be the friendliest canvas this project has ever had: 320×200 — exactly the layout every port shares — with a straight byte-per-pixel framebuffer (no bitplanes, no attribute clash, no tile maps) and 256 colours. So the PC build takes the Atari TT edition's gradient treatment and runs it as its everyday look: a 64-shade night-to-amber dusk behind the ziggurat, cell faces lit top-to-bottom through a 32-shade ramp, sand fading into the horizon. Sound is the humble PC speaker doing its one-voice best — the 3,400-year-old Hurrian Hymn as pure square waves off timer channel 2, plus the usual dice-clatter and capture sounds.

Going online, DOS style

How does a 1980s PC reach a modern game server? With a FujiNet, of course — on the PC it's the RS-232 flavour, an ESP32 device that hangs off a COM port. A little driver (FUJINET.SYS, from the FujiNet MS-DOS project) loads in CONFIG.SYS and exposes the whole thing through a software interrupt, INT F5h — very much in the spirit of every DOS network stack ever. The game calls the interrupt, the driver frames it over the serial port, the FujiNet does TCP to the internet, and suddenly your DOS box is trading 21-byte state packets with a Go server.

[ add screenshot: 29-dos-lobby.png ]

The full lobby came along: set your name, set your server, browse the leaderboard — your profile even lives on the FujiNet itself, in the same AppKey the Atari, Adam, C64, and Apple II builds share, so your name and win count follow you between machines. No FujiNet attached? The Online option politely tells you what's missing and the game plays locally like any other port.

Proving it without touching a screwdriver

Here's the part I enjoyed far too much. The whole online path was proven with no vintage hardware at all: FreeDOS booting inside DOSBox-X, the real FUJINET.SYS driver loaded from a floppy image, the emulator's COM port bridged over TCP to FujiNet-PC (the firmware compiled as a desktop program, pretending to be the RS-232 adapter), and from there out to the live server. Every layer is the genuine article — the driver banner even reads the firmware version over the wire and sets the DOS clock from the FujiNet's NTP before the game ever starts.

And then: a server-authoritative game of Ur, on DOS, against the server's computer opponent — the server rolls, the DOS box picks moves, the opponent's pieces march down their lane, captures knock beads back to the tray. Same server, same protocol, same games the other four online platforms play. If you have a FujiNet RS-232 and a real PC, it should be exactly a CONFIG.SYS edit away.

Also in v0.1.7

Everything that landed since the last release rides along on every channel:

  • Genesis: a real FM hymn. The title theme moved from the PSG to the YM2612 — a plucked-lyre patch programmed register by register, each note ringing and decaying like a string. (An FFT caught my first patch producing only odd harmonics — a clarinet, not a lyre. The things you learn about FM synthesis from a Bronze Age melody.)
  • Amiga: the blitter and a three-voice arrangement. Board redraws went from a visible crawl to a blink, and Paula now plays the hymn as a plucked melody with an octave double and a deep drone underneath — proper accompaniment for the oldest written song.
  • Captured pieces walk home. On the Genesis, Master System, and Game Gear, a captured token now visibly glides back to its owner's tray instead of teleporting.
  • Apple II double-hi-res fix. A linker-config bug that only newer cc65 releases catch — the build's variables were being squeezed into a negative-sized memory region. Two months of red CI, one line of config.

Sixteen machines, four CPU families — 6502, Z80, 68000, and now x86 — five of them online against one server. The board is 4,600 years old; it has never been this connected. As ever: it's free, it's GPL, and the source (including the whole DOS FujiNet writeup) is on GitHub.
